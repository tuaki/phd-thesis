\section{Self-adaptation}
\label{sec:self-adaptation}

With the categorical representation of multi-model data and the capabilities for querying and evolution, we have a solid foundation for managing multi-model data. Now we can focus on optimizing it.

Before developing our own search-based solution, we first studied a narrower but closely related workload-aware optimization problem at the physical level, namely index selection. In \paperRef{dke_2026}, we surveyed state-of-the-art index-selection methods, compared how major database systems implement and use indexes, and evaluated how far existing approaches transfer from relational systems to \acrshort{nosql}, multi-model, and multi-modal\footnote{Multi-modal systems combine different \emph{modalities} (e.g., text, image, video, and geospatial data).} settings. This gave us a concrete picture of what current automated tuning methods can and cannot do once the logical representation is already fixed.

\subsection{Index Optimization}

The survey begins by noting that index selection is one of the most important physical design decisions for workload performance, yet the existing literature remains primarily focused on relational databases. We therefore analyzed representative relational, graph, key-value, document, wide-column, multi-model, and multi-modal systems and compared both the index types they support and the workloads for which these indexes are useful. Based on that background, we reviewed state-of-the-art index-selection algorithms and assessed their broader applicability outside the relational setting.

The main conclusion was not that existing methods can be transferred directly, but rather that such transfer is limited and uneven. Many algorithms assume capabilities that are natural in relational systems but unavailable or much weaker elsewhere, such as rich what-if optimization interfaces, stable cost models, or a fairly uniform space of supported index types. At the same time, the survey confirmed that heterogeneous systems still face the same underlying need: they must choose workload-aware physical structures under storage and maintenance constraints, often for much more diverse query patterns than those addressed in classical relational tuning.

Two observations from that paper were especially important for the present chapter. First, cost estimation is the central technical bottleneck. Existing index-selection methods depend heavily on the ability to estimate the effects of many alternative configurations cheaply, without fully deploying them. Second, physical tuning alone is not enough for our setting. The survey assumes that the logical representation and database placement are already given, whereas in a multi-model environment, these choices may themselves dominate performance. This led us to ask whether the optimization problem should be formulated at a higher level of abstraction, over alternative logical representations of the same conceptual schema.

In that sense, \paperRef{dke_2026} serves primarily as motivation and inspiration. It showed us both the value of workload-aware automated optimization and the limits of staying at the purely physical level. Therefore, we decided that, instead of selecting indexes for a single fixed representation, we should search over alternative mappings, embeddings, and redundancies of the same conceptual schema across multiple databases. The richer physical-design space identified in \questionRef{sa}{richer-state-space} remains important future work, but it is not the main point of our optimization approach.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

We assume a fixed conceptual schema, a workload (a set of queries weighted by their frequencies), and a set of available database applications. Let us define a \term{configuration} as a set of logical schemas of these databases that, if materialized with the data, would allow the system to answer all queries in the workload. The goal is to find a configuration that minimizes the overall query latency. Based on this problem definition and the insights from the survey, we identified the following open problems:

\defineQuestionPrefix{sa}{A}

\question{sa}{search-space}{Search space}{The space of possible configurations is, in principle, unbounded and, even after removing obviously equivalent or strictly worse alternatives, still grows exponentially with the size of the conceptual schema. The system, therefore, needs a deterministic finite representation that still captures the main structural choices relevant to performance, especially model assignment, embedding, and cross-model redundancy, together with their maintenance costs.}

\question{sa}{search-strategy}{Search strategy}{Even in a finite state space, the number of configurations grows too quickly for exhaustive evaluation, so the system must explore alternatives systematically while balancing exploitation and exploration. This is particularly important in multi-model settings, where small changes often result in cross-model queries. Those are generally more expensive than single-model ones, thus creating local maxima that can be escaped only by larger structural changes.}

\question{sa}{latency-estimation}{Latency estimation}{To evaluate a configuration, the system has to at least estimate the latency of the queries in the workload. A naive solution (executing the queries) is too expensive. A much more efficient approach is required to make the search process feasible. Specifically, this solution cannot require the actual materialization of the configuration, as that, by itself, is too expensive.}

\question{sa}{cross-model}{Cross-model queries}{Given the nature of the multi-model environment, some queries may require data from multiple models. Therefore, the system has to be able to estimate the latency of cross-model queries, taking into account the cost of data transfers and transformations between models.}

\question{sa}{migration-cost}{Migration cost}{Migration to the final configuration is usually very expensive, as it may involve large data transfers and downtime. The performance gain of the new configuration must be significant enough to justify the migration cost. The system has to be able to estimate the cost of migrating to (and maintaining) a configuration.}
    
% \question{sa}{adaptation-timing}{Adaptation timing}{After a better configuration is identified and the cost of migration is estimated, it is still unclear when the adaptation should be executed. Immediate adaptation may react quickly to workload changes but incur excessive migration overhead, while delayed adaptation may miss important optimization opportunities. The system should therefore decide when the expected long-term benefit outweighs the large one-time migration cost.}

\question{sa}{workload-prediction}{Workload prediction}{In practice, workloads are not static. They evolve over time and may contain both periodic trends and one-off anomalies. A truly autonomous system should reason not only about the present workload but also about its likely future development, so it can avoid overfitting to transient patterns and prepare for upcoming changes.}

\question{sa}{richer-state-space}{Richer search space}{Database applications offer a wide range of physical design choices beyond the logical model, such as indexes, materialized views, partitioning schemes, replication strategies, knob tuning, etc. These choices are not currently covered by the categorical representation, but they can significantly impact query performance.}

\subsection{MM-mapsearch}

Motivated by the open problems above and by the limitations revealed by the index-selection survey, we propose the following principles:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A \term{state} is an element of a finite discrete \term{state space} represented by a matrix. It maps the conceptual schema and the databases to a configuration. The state space is then a graph of all possible states connected by the available transitions.
    \item The state space is explored using an algorithm that evaluates the states both directly (by estimating the latency of the corresponding configurations) and indirectly (by the values of neighboring states).
    \item There is a model that, given a state and a workload, can cheaply estimate the total latency of the corresponding configuration without actually materializing it.
\end{itemize}
It is important to note that, while these principles are general and most likely applicable in other approaches to the problem of self-adaptation, they can be enabled only by a sufficiently robust representation of multi-model data.

In our approach, the categorical representation provides a unified view of the conceptual schema, the logical schemas, and the mappings between them. It allows us to describe queries (and thus the workload) independently of the actual configurations, as well as to reason about cross-model queries. It also defines a set of \acrshort{smo}s that provide a natural way to transform one configuration into another, which is crucial for defining state transitions. Lastly, we can use it to migrate data to the final configuration.

An in-depth description of the proposed approach, together with a proof-of-concept implementation called \term{MM-mapsearch}, is provided in \paperRef{edbt_2026}. The following sections contain a short summary and a few improvements we have made since the publication of that paper.

\subsubsection{State Space}

In our approach, we consider complex types, not simple types, to be the most basic building blocks. That is, if a complex type is mapped to a kind, all of its properties are mapped to the attributes of that kind. Then, a configuration tells us in which database(s) each kind is stored and into which other kind (if any) it is embedded. This limitation allows us to define a finite state space, as there is only a finite number of ways to assign the kinds to the available databases and to embed them into each other.

\paperRef{edbt_2026} describes a simple representation of a state consisting of two vectors, both of which have the same number of elements as there are complex types in the conceptual schema. The first vector contains the database to which each complex type is assigned. The second vector contains the kind into which each complex type is embedded (or \code{none} if it is not embedded into any other kind).

This representation of a state has one major issue: it does not support cross-model redundancy, so if kinds used in a query are stored across multiple databases, the query must be cross-model, resulting in a significant performance penalty. Such a penalty is likely to outweigh the benefits of using multiple databases, so the search process would lead to single-database configurations. To address this issue, we have replaced the previous two vectors with a \term{state matrix}. Its columns correspond to the complex types, while its rows correspond to the databases. The values are one of:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \code{0} (i.\,e., \code{false}), if the kind (for the given complex type) is not stored in this database at all,
    \item \code{1} (i.\,e., \code{true}), if the kind is stored in this database, but not embedded into any other kind, and
    \item the identifier of another kind, if the kind is stored in this database and embedded into that other kind.
\end{itemize}
This allows us to represent both cross-model redundancy and embedding while keeping the state space finite. The number of possible states is further reduced by a handful of constraints, such as that a kind cannot be embedded into itself, or that all complex types must be represented by at least one kind. The resulting state space is still large, but it is manageable and can be explored systematically.

\subsubsection{Search Strategy}

Although the state space is finite, it is still too large for exhaustive evaluation. We considered several search strategies, including greedy search, genetic algorithms, and integer linear programming. Ultimately, we chose \acrshort{mcts}, but we are still exploring other options.

The main idea behind \acrshort{mcts} is to incrementally build a tree of evaluated states. At each step, the algorithm first selects the most promising path from the root to a leaf node, balancing exploitation and exploration. Then, it expands the tree by applying a migration step to the selected leaf node, thereby creating a new child node. A corresponding configuration is evaluated using the latency estimation model, and the result is backpropagated up the tree to update the values of the nodes along the path.

A more detailed description of the algorithm can be found in \Cref{edbt_2026:sec:mcts}. The standard version of \acrshort{mcts} is designed for tree-structured search spaces (e.g., a game of chess), so we had to make some adjustments. For instance, we use a \acrshort{dag} instead of a tree, as the same configuration can be reached by different sequences of migration steps.

\begin{example}
\label{ex:aimm-search}
\Cref{fig:aimm-search} shows a simplified version of the searched \acrshort{dag}. The left part shows the initial state from \Cref{fig:aimm-state} (node with label 0) and all its possible modifications of \object{User}. That is, we can either remove it from PostgreSQL (and get to state 1), or add it to ClickHouse (2) or MongoDB (3). Notably, we cannot remove it from Neo4j, as that would require removing \object{Friend} first, because it is represented there as a relationship. For the same reason, we cannot embed it into \object{Friend} in Neo4j. Next, nodes 4--6 are all the states reachable by two consecutive modifications of \object{User}. \subsubsection{Latency Estimation}

Similarly to the search strategy, we treat latency estimation as a black-box function that can be implemented in various ways. So far, we have tried two approaches: a plan-structured neural network (see \Cref{edbt_2026:sec:neural-network}) and a random decision tree.

Both approaches estimate query latency from the query plan, which must be obtained from the database. Although current databases still require the configuration to be materialized in order to obtain the query plan, there is no fundamental reason why this should be the case. In general, databases generate query plans based on the schema and data statistics. This information can be obtained once (e.g., from the database where the data is currently stored) and then used as input to the planning engines of other databases.

Although this is still very much an open research question, we are optimistic that it can be solved in the near future, which would make the search process much more efficient. Until then, we can use explicit materialization to train, test, and validate the latency estimation models.\\

Once we can estimate the latency of queries in the individual databases, we can extend this estimation to cross-model queries by leveraging the query plans generated by the \acrshort{mmql} query engine. A simple cost model for \acrshort{mmql} queries was proposed and implemented in~\citep{strobl-mastersthesis}. It uses the estimated latencies of the individual subqueries, together with other metadata that can be obtained in a similar way (e.g., the size of the intermediate results), to estimate the overall latency of cross-model queries.\\

Lastly, we can also use the latency estimation model to estimate both the cost of migrating to a new configuration and the cost of maintaining it. Our approach is to define a set of \code{INSERT}, \code{UPDATE}, and \code{DELETE} queries that represent the expected workload of the migration and maintenance processes, and then estimate their latency in the same way as for the workload queries.

However, this is still a work in progress, as there are many other factors that can affect migration cost. For example, the cost of downtime depends not only on the latency of the migration queries, but also on the \uv{business} cost of doing so, which, in turn, depends heavily on the specific application and its users. Nevertheless, the proposed cost model is a good starting point in the search for a more general solution to this problem, as it is able to provide all of the \uv{technical} information needed to estimate migration cost, while leaving the \uv{business} part to the user.

\begin{example}
\label{ex:aimm-result}
Currently, the workload consists of two queries (\Cref{ex:querying-single,ex:querying-multi}). Neither of them uses the \object{Friend} complex type, so the algorithm will try to optimize it away. We can fix that by adding a third query that uses \object{Friend}, but for the sake of this example, we will keep the workload as it is.

The precise result is impossible to predict, as it depends on the actual latencies of the queries in the individual databases. However, we can make an educated guess that the algorithm will try to move both \object{User} and \object{Review} to the same database, as that would eliminate the cross-model query. Thus, the final configuration will most likely consist of two independent parts---the fastest database for the query from \Cref{ex:querying-single} (containing \object{Business} and \object{Attribute}) and the fastest database for the query from \Cref{ex:querying-multi} (\object{User} and \object{Review}).
\end{example}

In real-world scenarios, both data and workloads are much more complex than in \Cref{ex:aimm-result}. Such a clear separation of the workload into independent parts is quite rare, so the process will usually result in either several cross-model queries or redundant kinds. The balance between these two options is determined by the relative overhead of cross-model querying vs. the additional cost of migration and maintenance of redundant data.

\subsection{Related Work}

We explored related approaches from three directions. First, physical-design methods such as index selection, materialized-view selection, and related forms of workload-aware tuning optimize performance by modifying structures inside one database system (and usually one logical model). As already discussed in \Cref{sec:foundations-related-work,sec:evolution-related-work}, adjacent work also exists on multi-model representation and evolution. What is still missing, however, is a formulation in which the choice of logical model, embedding, and redundancy becomes the optimization space itself.

This difference matters because the logical representation alone can have a major impact on workload latency~\citep{10.1145/3183713.3199671}. In a multi-model setting, the problem is even harder---the same conceptual data may be placed in different stores, embedded in different ways, or duplicated across models, and each such choice changes not only local operator costs but also the need for cross-model execution. Traditional physical-design approaches usually assume that the model and storage platform are already fixed. Our setting starts one level above that assumption and treats alternative mappings themselves as the objects to be compared.

The second relevant direction is workload-aware and learning-based tuning. Recent work shows that learned methods can support latency prediction, data-structure design, or system-parameter tuning~\citep{10.14778/3342263.3342646, 10.1145/3183713.3199671,10.1155/2023/8835111,10.1007/s10844-022-00762-0}. These approaches are highly relevant to our problem because they show how search can be guided by predicted workload behavior rather than by hand-crafted heuristics alone. At the same time, they usually optimize an existing configuration (e.g., they tune knobs, choose physical structures, or estimate plan costs), but they do not explicitly search over heterogeneous mappings of one conceptual schema across several database models.

The third relevant direction is multistore and polystore research, which addresses interoperability and query execution across heterogeneous backends~\citep{alotaibi2020estocada, DBLP:journals/ijcc/BondiombouyV16, 10.1145/3417990.3421999}. These systems show how different stores can be combined within one application and how queries can cross system boundaries. However, they typically treat the available placement of data as given, or at least outside the main optimization problem. Likewise, evolution-oriented approaches such as Darwin~\citep{storl2022darwin}, MigCast~\citep{hillenbrand2019migcast}, and related \acrshort{nosql} migration work, as well as the categorical and logical-level approaches discussed in the previous chapter, are highly relevant as enabling technology, but they primarily address migration, schema modification, or impact analysis rather than workload-driven search over alternative multi-model layouts.

Our approach combines elements that these lines of work usually keep separate. It assumes a fixed conceptual schema, makes mappings first-class objects, defines an explicit search space of alternative configurations, reasons about migration between them, and evaluates candidates with respect to a weighted workload. In contrast to classical physical-design tuning, the search space here includes not only indexes or access paths, but also model assignment, embedding, and cross-model redundancy.

\subsection{Contributions}

We conducted a survey of index selection in heterogeneous database systems (\paperRef{dke_2026}). This survey compared indexing strategies across relational, graph, key-value, document, wide-column, multi-model, and multi-modal systems, assessed how existing index-selection methods transfer beyond the relational setting, and identified the main obstacles to such transfer. In particular, it showed that current approaches depend heavily on cost-estimation capabilities and usually assume that the logical representation is already fixed, which helped us delimit the scope of existing physical-design methods and motivated the search for a higher-level optimization approach.

We formulated self-adaptation as a workload-aware optimization problem over the logical schemas and their mappings to a fixed conceptual schema. Instead of treating different data models resp. databases individually, our approach represents alternative configurations of the same conceptual schema in a common search space. This makes it possible to compare heterogeneous designs systematically and to reason about adaptation at the level of the whole multi-model system rather than one store at a time (\paperRef{edbt_2026}).

The categorical framework allowed us to define a state representation that captures the essential aspects of a configuration, including model assignment, embedding, and cross-model redundancy. This representation is both expressive enough to cover the most important design choices and compact enough to keep the search space finite (\questionRef{sa}{search-space}). It also provides a principled way to migrate data between configurations.

On top of this representation, we introduced an automated optimization process that derives configuration changes from weighted workloads and explores them using \acrshort{mcts}. This algorithm enables us to systematically explore the search space, favors promising regions, and reduces the risk of being trapped in local maxima (\questionRef{sa}{search-strategy}).

Because evaluating every configuration directly on the databases would be too expensive, we complement the search with learned performance estimation. In particular, we extend a plan-structured neural predictor~\citep{10.14778/3342263.3342646} for non-relational databases, so that configurations can be evaluated solely from the query plans of the workload queries (\questionRef{sa}{latency-estimation}). However, a method that would not require full materialization to obtain query plans remains future work. A similar approach is used to estimate cross-model queries (\questionRef{sa}{cross-model}), as well as the cost of migrating to and maintaining a configuration (\questionRef{sa}{migration-cost}).

Lastly, we implemented these ideas in a proof-of-concept prototype MM-mapsearch that integrates workload analysis, generation of schema variants, neural latency prediction, and an interactive interface for inspecting the recommended alternatives. Together, these results provide an initial practical foundation for automatic adaptation of multi-model data layouts under evolving workloads.
